# Starfarer

A real-time, seamless, scale-free explorer of the real and procedurally generated universe — a
SpaceEngine-style experience for desktop and VR headsets, built on **real astronomical data** (JPL
ephemerides, AT-HYG / HYG / Gaia DR3 stars, JPL satellite and small-body catalogues, NASA/USGS
planetary maps, the NASA Exoplanet Archive, JPL Horizons spacecraft trajectories, SIMBAD galaxies)
with deterministic procedural generation for everything the catalogues don't cover.

**What you can visit:** the Solar System with 16k close-up maps of 15 worlds · 2.75 million catalogued
stars, each with its own surface, plus billions of generated ones filling the Milky Way · **6,333
confirmed exoplanets** in 4,743 systems and a generated planetary system around (almost) every other
star · 23 black holes with lensing, accretion disks and jets · **real spacecraft** at their real
positions (ISS, Hubble, JWST, Voyager 1 & 2, New Horizons, Parker Solar Probe, Europa Clipper, Juice,
Lucy, Psyche) · **47 nearby galaxies** (Andromeda, Triangulum, the Magellanic Clouds, the Whirlpool,
the Sombrero, Centaurus A, M87 …) · **41 nebulae and star clusters** (the Orion, Carina, Eagle,
Ring, Helix and Crab nebulae, the Pleiades, Omega Centauri, 47 Tucanae …) · the Milky Way from outside ·
**real 3D ground to land on**: NASA elevation models for the Moon, Mars, Mercury and Ceres and
NOAA's for Earth, with a tour of landmarks to fly to (Mount Everest, the Grand Canyon, Kilimanjaro, the
Matterhorn, Mauna Kea, Mount Fuji, Denali, Olympus Mons, Valles Marineris, the Curiosity, Perseverance,
Spirit and Opportunity sites, the Apollo 11, 15 and 17 sites, Chang'e 4 on the far side, Tycho,
Copernicus, Shackleton at the lunar south pole, the Caloris Basin, Occator and Ahuna Mons on
Ceres), generated hills, craters
and shadows on other moons, and on rocky planets of other stars (their mountains and seas match their
colours; temperate worlds have blue skies) · **inside Saturn's
rings** among their ice (search "rings") · comets near the Sun with comas, tails and (up close) a
dark nucleus with jets · **eclipses at their
real times** (moon shadows crossing Jupiter, the Moon turning copper in Earth's shadow).

**The ship (V, or More → Spaceship on a phone):** a ship that obeys physics all the way up — as
fast as you like, never faster than light. Its motion is Einstein's special relativity, exactly (to
Lorentz factors of a billion and more), under the real gravity of every world near it; its clock
runs slow against the universe's, so on a long trip the date runs on and the planets move. Its
autopilot (J, or **Go there** while aboard) flies flip-and-burn: accelerate, turn, brake, and arrive
at rest beside the target, going round any world in the way; to Alpha Centauri at 1 g it takes
about 4 years aboard and 6 in the universe, to the outskirts of the Andromeda Galaxy at 1,000 g under three weeks
aboard and 2.3 million years in the universe. What is invented is the engine: a "reactionless
drive" whose push the pilot sets from a thousandth of a g to a million g and which needs no
propellant (no real engine can do without reaction mass), and that the crew does not feel it. A
realistic fusion torch (1.3 g, 25 km/s in all) is the other drive. Also: a cockpit with live
navigation screens (sit inside it in VR), a chase view, fictional traffic and a rotating space
station orbiting the world you are near (fly up to its docking port and the docking computer takes
over), landing on solid worlds, missions and a discovery log.

**Gravity for real (tap a world: Mass, Move, Add, Remove):** the Solar System is the real one,
where the ephemeris has it, until you change something in it. From that moment it is simulated:
everything in it pulls on everything else, and one button, **Back to reality**, undoes it all.
**You see what happens**: after each change time speeds up for fifteen seconds, to a pace at which
the worlds it matters to go round, and the view eases out to frame them ("Watching: 26 minutes a
second", with Stop; then **Replay**). **Undo** steps back one change at a time, the date with it. Make
Jupiter twenty times heavier and its moons fall onto new orbits, some of them into it. Take the Sun
away and the planets leave on straight lines, in the dark. Drag the Moon out to twice its distance
and within four months the Sun has taken it from the Earth. **Throw** a world: pull back from it like
a slingshot, its arrow marked where it would go round in a circle and where it would escape, or flick
it. Add a second Jupiter near the Earth's orbit, or a second sun: put it where you want it with a
finger, give it the mass you want, from a small moon to two suns (its size follows from its mass:
rock as dense as the Earth, a giant as big as Jupiter, a star of the main sequence), let go or flick
it, and Done sets it going. Time runs from real time up to a thousand years a second (as fast as the
simulation manages, and it says how fast that is); what collides becomes one; what happens is told
in words and kept in a log. What theory predicts of a body of the mass you gave it is labelled as
a prediction, and what the simulation leaves out is listed in the app, two taps from the mark that
says physics is live.

## Quick start

```bash
npm install
npm run dev          # http://127.0.0.1:5173
```

All processed data (≈400 MB, most of it close-up map tiles) is committed under `public/data/`, so the app runs without the
pipeline. To rebuild every data file from the original sources, see [`pipeline/`](pipeline/README.md)
(`npm run data`, needs Python 3.10+ and ~0.5 GB of downloads).

Production build: `npm run build && npm run preview`.
Deploy to GitHub Pages: automatic. `.github/workflows/deploy-pages.yml` runs `npm run check`
(typecheck, unit tests, production build) on every push to `main` and, if it passes, publishes
`dist/` to the `gh-pages` branch, which GitHub Pages serves (**Settings → Pages → Build and
deployment → Source: Deploy from a branch → `gh-pages` / `(root)`**).

Starfarer began as a copy of [Space Explorer](https://github.com/dippy34/nextjs-boilerplate) (same
owner) without its God mode (the universe editor and N-body sandbox), which is not included yet.

### Controls

**On a phone or tablet** it works like an app: two chips at the top (where you are, what time it
is), a tab bar under your thumb (Search · Explore · Time · More) and one panel at a time above it.
Add it to the home screen to open it without the browser around it.

![The interface on a phone: Saturn's card, places to go, search, standing on the Moon, and a phone on its side](docs/img/phone.jpg)

| Touch | Action |
|---|---|
| Drag | Turn round the world you are at — its surface follows your finger (close up, the ground slides under it) |
| Pinch | Zoom, all the way down to standing height; what is between your fingers stays there |
| Two-finger drag | Look around (near the ground the horizon stays level) |
| Twist | Roll the view |
| Tap | Select what you tapped (or the name beside it) · tap empty sky to let go |
| Double-tap | Fly there; on the world you are already at, come six times closer to the spot you tapped |
| **Drag: orbit / Drag: look** button | Says what one finger does now (turn round the world, or look around); a tap changes it |
| **Your ship** / **Fly there in your ship** | Board the spaceship; with something selected, fly there in it (the drive set strong enough for the trip) |

**More** starts with **How to move** (every touch), the **Missions** (goals to fly to, and how many
are done) and the **Start menu**. After a long trip the **Time** panel offers **Back to today**; a
flight to a place on the ground runs in real time (fast time would turn the place away under it).

In the ship (the ship button, or More → Spaceship):

| Touch | Action |
|---|---|
| Drag | Turn the ship: the sky goes with your finger, as fast as the ship's reaction wheels allow; let go and it holds its attitude |
| Twist · Pinch | Roll the ship · zoom the view |
| The thrust lever (at the right; on its side, along the foot) | Slide up (on its side: right) for more thrust, to the end for none; it stays where you leave it |
| **Stop** | Turn round and brake to rest beside the nearest world or star (where it pulls, the drive then holds you there) |
| **Chase / Cockpit** | See the ship from behind, or from its cockpit |
| The drive's name · − · + | Fusion torch or reactionless drive · the reactionless drive's setting, from 0.001 g to 1,000,000 g |
| The instruments (under the clock) | Tap for the full read-out |
| Tap · Double-tap · **Go there** | Select · the autopilot flies there, never faster than light |

Any control (a drag, the lever) takes the ship back from the autopilot or from Stop.

The card of what you selected has **Go there**, **Look at** and **Details**; nothing moves the
camera until you go. A flight in progress shows a **Stop** button. Under them, for a world of the
Solar System, is what you can do to it: **Mass**, **Move**, **Add**, **Remove** (gravity for real,
above); the first use switches live physics on, and at the top **Undo** steps back a change at a
time and **Back to reality** undoes it all (itself undone, for a moment, by Undo). In Move, choose
**Throw**, then pull back from the world and let go, or flick it. After Add, drag the new world where
you want it, set its mass, and tap **Done**. A finger on the view while a change is being watched
gives you the camera back; More → *Show me what happens* turns the watching off.

**With a mouse and keyboard** the same interface stands at the left edge, and every key still works:

| 0–9 | Sun, Mercury … Pluto |
| Space · `[` `]` · `\` · Backspace | Pause · slower/faster time · reverse · real time now |
| Ctrl+Z | Undo the last change to a world |
| L · O · M | Names · orbits · minor-body orbits |
| P · H · Esc | Screenshot · help · stop autopilot / close a panel / deselect |
| V · J | Spaceship (cockpit / chase / off) · in it, the autopilot to the selection |
| = · - · I | In the ship: the reactionless drive's setting up / down · torch or reactionless drive |
| K · N | Missions and discoveries · ship sound on/off |
| T | Tour: Saturn's rings, Olympus Mons, the Apollo 11 site, an eclipse, a comet, a black hole … |
| U | Photo mode: hide the interface and names (P saves a PNG) |

**Walking on a phone:** over solid ground a **Land and walk** button comes down to stand on it (or
take the tour's walk at Apollo 17). Then the stick under your left thumb walks the way you push it
(all the way: run), a finger dragged on the view looks around, **Jump** jumps (1.9 m on the Moon)
and **Fly** takes off again. Arriving at a place where it is night, a notice says so with the time
to sunrise and offers **Skip to sunrise**; the tour's places are arrived at in daylight.

Flying freely still needs the keyboard (or a headset); its touch controls come next.
On a phone the ship is flown by hand (the table above) or by its autopilot (Search, then Go there).
Its instruments stand under the clock: the speed with its nines, the Lorentz factor, what the ship is doing and the
time to the target in both clocks; a tap on them opens the full read-out. In the ship the Time
chip shows the ship's own time warp (×1 to ×100,000, Time → Faster), as it actually runs, and the
Time sheet how fast the universe's clock runs with it.

**Landing:** go to a solid world (the Moon, Mars, Mercury, Ganymede, Callisto … or a rocky planet of
another star) and keep descending (F; in VR, point the left controller down and push the left stick): below about 40 km the sphere turns
into real 3D ground, and you can fly down to standing height. In ship mode, coming down slowly below
10 m is a touchdown. For long shadows, come down near the terminator (the day/night line).

### VR (WebXR) — built for the headset

![VR menu, Io, the Moon, the Milky Way, Earth and Saturn](docs/img/vr-and-visuals.jpg)

Open the link in the headset's browser (Meta Quest Browser, or Chrome/Edge on a PC with a Link /
SteamVR headset) and press **ENTER VR**. The view fades in and the **menu** opens in front of you.

* **Menu** (Y on the left controller, or MENU on your wrist): tabs for Planets, Moons (by planet),
  Small worlds (dwarf planets, big asteroids, famous comets), Stars, Exoplanets, Nebulae, Galaxies,
  Craft, **Places** (highlights, then landmarks by world: Earth, the Moon, Mars, Mercury; Saturn's
  rings, eclipses), **Search** (virtual keyboard over
  every planet, moon, asteroid, comet and 12,585 named stars) and Settings. Every item shows a
  thumbnail rendered from the real map and its live distance; **point and pull the trigger** to fly there.
* **Travel** blinks, turns the view so the destination is straight ahead, then flies there in a
  straight line with a comfort vignette and parks you where the world fills your view ("Blink" travel in
  Settings jumps instead).
* **Point at anything in the sky**: a ring and its name/distance appear (with a haptic tick); the trigger
  selects it and opens an info card; pull the trigger on it again, press **A**, or "Fly to" to go there.

| Controller | Action |
|---|---|
| Trigger (either hand) | Click menus · select what the laser points at · again on the selection: fly there |
| A · B | Fly to the selection · back (stop flight / close card / close menu / deselect) |
| Y · X | Menu · pause / resume time |
| Left stick · left grip | Fly where the left controller points (speed scales with altitude) · ×10 |
| Right stick | Turn (snap 30° or smooth, in Settings) · up/down: flight speed |
| Right grip + right stick | Orbit around / zoom to the selection |
| Stick clicks | Right: labels on/off · left: real time, now |

Hand tracking (no controllers) and gaze-and-pinch work the same way: pinch = trigger. The left wrist
shows the date, time rate, selection and MENU / FLY TO / PAUSE buttons. Settings: labels, orbit lines
(off by default in VR), travel and turning style, Milky Way and star brightness, time controls, exit.

Frames go straight into the headset's stereo framebuffer (tone mapping inside every material, the
same ACES curve as the desktop), logarithmic depth, a glare sprite around the Sun instead of bloom,
and a pull-in projection for runtimes that clamp the far plane. Tested with Meta's IWER WebXR emulator
(Quest 3 profile, stereo, projection layers, controllers and hands — `npm run verify:vr`, 23 checks
including "every VR draw lands in the headset framebuffer"); not yet profiled on headset hardware.

URL parameters (also used by the automated tests): `time=2026-10-01T20:00:00Z`, `rate=86400`,
`paused=1`, `target=Saturn&dist=6&az=40&el=20` (distance in radii), `look=Betelgeuse`,
`campc=x,y,z` (camera position in parsecs), `fov=60`, `starlimit=7.5`, `gaia=0` (skip the
non-commercial Gaia dataset), `depth=log` (force logarithmic depth), `xr=0` (never offer VR),
`mw=1` (Milky Way brightness, 0 hides it).

## Why Three.js (WebGL2) + TypeScript + Vite

* **Streaming is native to the web.** The star catalogue is an octree of ~1,200 binary tiles and the
  ephemeris is chunked by decade; the browser fetches exactly what the camera needs. A desktop shell
  (Tauri/Electron) can wrap it later without changing the engine.
* **Full shader control without boilerplate.** Three.js gives render targets, HDR formats, and both
  reversed-Z (`EXT_clip_control`) and logarithmic depth, while every material here is a hand-written
  GLSL `ShaderMaterial` (stars, PSF sprites, planets, rings, GPU Kepler propagation).
* **Verifiable here.** Each phase is run and checked in headless Chromium (Playwright + SwiftShader);
  that rules out Unity/Unreal in this environment, and makes Rust/wgpu (Bevy) iteration much slower.
* Babylon.js would also work; Three.js is lighter and its WebGPU path is available later for compute.

## Architecture

```
src/
  core/      units, double-double universal positions (UPos), time scales, reference frames
  astro/     DE442S chunk evaluator, Kepler/universal propagation, IAU rotation models, photometry
  universe/  Solar System (planets, 459 moons, minor bodies), streaming star catalogue, named stars
  render/    HDR renderer (bloom + ACES), star field, Milky Way, bodies (planets, rings, Sun, relief,
             glint), atmospheres, orbits, GPU asteroids, comets (Web Worker), near stars, labels
  sim/       physics: the N-body engine of live physics (NBody.ts), the ship's relativity, the
             textbook formulas behind the worked-out facts
  app/       main loop, camera rig (free fly / orbit / autopilot), input, WebXR (VR.ts), live
             physics and the powers (LivePhysics.ts, Powers.ts, Mover.ts, WhatIf.ts)
  vr/        in-headset UI: canvas panels with hit regions, the explorer menu
  workers/   comet propagation (double precision, off the main thread)
pipeline/    Python: download → validate → convert raw catalogues into public/data/
tests/       unit tests incl. comparison against JPL Horizons reference vectors
scripts/     headless browser verification (scenario screenshots, interaction checks)
trailer/     the trailer: frame-by-frame capture of real footage, the edit, the score (trailer/README.md)
```

**Scale and precision.** Every object has a double-double position (≈32 significant digits, i.e.
millimetres at 100 Mpc). Each frame, positions are differenced against the camera in double-double,
converted to doubles and only then to float32 for the GPU — a floating origin, so the camera always
sits at (0,0,0). Depth uses a reversed-Z float32 buffer (logarithmic fallback). The camera co-moves
with whichever body's sphere of influence it is in, so time acceleration never leaves you behind.

**Photometry.** All light is in one physical unit system (radiance 1 = white Lambertian surface lit by
the Sun at 1 AU). Stars, unresolved planets, asteroids and comets are drawn as point-spread sprites
whose integrated value equals the physical irradiance at the camera, so a planet fading from disk to
dot keeps its brightness. Eye adaptation follows the brightest resolved disk in view; a perceptual
power law is applied to point sources (as SpaceEngine/Celestia do).

**Real until touched.** The Solar System is where the JPL ephemeris has it until a power is used
on it. The first change hands the bodies that matter to an N-body simulation, started from where
each is and how it moves at that date; from then on the simulation keeps the time and says what
each body goes round, and "Back to reality" gives everything back to the ephemeris
(`docs/notes/gravity-engine.md`).

**Stars.** A brightest-first octree: each node holds the intrinsically brightest stars of its cube not
taken by an ancestor, so "could any star in this subtree be visible from here?" is an exact test and
tiles stream in without popping. Colours come from Planck spectra integrated against CIE colour
matching functions.

## Status

### What works (each verified before it went live)

Checked with `npm test` and the headless-browser suites (`scripts/verify.mjs`, `start.mjs`,
`touch.mjs`, `interact.mjs`, `exposure.mjs`, `stars.mjs`, `whatif.mjs`, `live.mjs`, `vr.mjs`,
`game.mjs`, `walk.mjs`, `terrain.mjs`, `places.mjs`, `flight.mjs`; see HANDOFF.md):

* **A star up close has a face**, drawn the way the space observatories show the Sun's (SDO,
  STEREO: the orange pictures of a star that everyone knows): glowing plasma, mottled and threaded
  all over, deeper in the star's colour where it is darker and white-hot where it is brightest;
  active regions; a red skin of light at the limb with prominences standing on it; a corona; all
  of it seething in place and turning with the star. Each kind has its own: a red dwarf deep red
  with vast active regions, a red supergiant in a handful of giant cells, hot stars blue-white
  and nearly smooth. **The Sun wears its own face**: a whole-sphere map of the real Sun in the
  extreme ultraviolet, put together by NASA from the three spacecraft that saw all of it at once
  in 2011, its real active regions with loops standing on them (`src/render/StarLook.ts`,
  `shaders/star.ts`, `pipeline/build_sun_map.py`, `scripts/stars.mjs`).

* **Live physics: gravity for real** (`src/app/LivePhysics.ts` on `src/sim/NBody.ts`;
  `scripts/live.mjs`, `docs/notes/gravity-engine.md`). The real Solar System stays real until a
  power is used on it; someone who never touches one gets the same pictures and the same frame as
  before. The powers are on a world's card, under its three buttons: **Mass** (a slider from a
  thousandth to a thousand times its real mass: "2 Jupiters", "0.5 Suns"; the orbits answer while
  the thumb is still down; what theory predicts of that mass is labelled a prediction), **Move**
  (a finger on the world drags it in the plane of its orbit while time waits; where it is left it
  keeps its velocity or is put on a circle; or it is thrown: pulled back from like a slingshot, an
  arrow marked at the speed of a circle and of escape, the orbit it would be on drawn while it is
  aimed, or flicked, as fast as the finger went: `src/app/Mover.ts`), **Add** (a moon for a planet;
  for a star a planet like the Earth, one like Jupiter, a second sun; put in place with a finger
  while time waits, its mass set from a small moon to two suns and its size following from it by
  stated rules, the working in its details; Done sets it going: bodies like any other from then on,
  which say they were made) and **Remove** (any world, the Sun included). The first change starts an N-body
  simulation from where everything is at that date (Wisdom and Holman's symplectic mapping on a
  hierarchy of pairs that is found, not given; exact two-body motion for every pair; collisions
  that merge; changes of what goes round what): the Sun, the planets, Pluto, Ceres, the weighed
  asteroids and the twenty moons heavy enough to matter, and any other world once it or its planet
  is touched. A mark says physics is live, with **Back to reality** beside it; the Time sheet has
  the log of what happened (which two collided and how fast, who was thrown out, who was captured)
  and the list of what the simulation leaves out. Orbit lines are drawn from where each body is
  and how it moves now, with a trail of where it has been. Each change is watched (`src/app/Watch.ts`):
  time runs for fifteen seconds at the pace at which the quickest orbit among the worlds whose pull
  it changes by a hundredth or more goes round in eight, and the camera eases to a frame that holds
  them, in the part of the view the interface leaves clear; then the rate comes back as it was, and
  Replay watches it again from just after the change (the simulation goes the same way). **Undo**
  goes back a change at a time, twenty deep, each to the last bit (`src/app/History.ts`,
  `LivePhysics.checkpoint`); Back to reality returns to the moment the first change was made, and
  can itself be undone for a moment. A system with no sun, or with two, is
  lit accordingly. Time: up to a year a second in full with every planet there, about a hundred
  years a second when more is asked, a thousand with the Sun gone (one core, a quarter of each
  frame). Checked by tests against the textbook (a moon's period after its planet's mass is
  doubled, the straight lines after the Sun is gone, the speed of a fall at contact) and through
  the interface on a phone-sized screen with fingers. Not in the headset's menus yet.

* **What if** (the beginning of it): a world's details show what follows from its facts by the
  textbook (its gravity, its escape velocity, how long its orbit takes, a black hole's horizon),
  and every line opens to its working: the formula, the numbers put in, the result. A planet can be
  given a moon (the card's **Add**): sized to it, on a circular orbit between you and the planet,
  with the period Kepler's third law gives it; it is simulated from the moment it is made, and from
  then on a body like any other (drawn, named, listed, flown to, pulling on a ship, casting its
  shadow), which says that it was made and is removed like any world. No mode to switch on: each
  of these is a button where it belongs (`src/app/Worked.ts`, `src/app/WhatIf.ts`,
  `scripts/whatif.mjs`).

* The eye adapts like one: it shuts down at once for something bright, opens over a moment in the
  dark, and holds its exposure for as long as any of a bright thing is in the frame, so looking
  away from the Sun, a planet's rings or a black hole's disk, and back, never flashes
  (`src/app/Exposure.ts`, `scripts/exposure.mjs`). A sun in the frame puts out the stars but not
  the worlds beside it; a sun behind a world holds nothing (in a planet's shadow the stars are
  out and its night side is there to be seen); a far-off accretion disk is a steady glint, the
  same coming and going, that grows into the disk as you come near, not a ball of light that
  swells and shrinks; and inside a galaxy its light is a glow with a brighter middle, not a fog.
* Stars are sharp points on any screen: each one's light is summed over the pixels it falls on, at
  the screen's own density; the bright ones are white-hot points in a soft glow, not disks.

* Seamless scale from metres above a planet to kiloparsecs, floating origin, reversed-Z depth.
* 2,751,164 real stars streamed from two separately-licensed octrees (AT-HYG+HYG; Gaia DR3 100 pc
  supplement), physical brightness and blackbody colours, 12,585 named stars, picking with real
  designations (HIP/HD/TYC/Gaia DR3), stars resolve into disks with a face of their own when you fly to them.
* Solar System at the real date: JPL DE442S (1849–2150; validated against JPL Horizons to ≲50 km for
  Earth, 1 km for the Moon), JPL approximate elements outside that range, IAU rotation models (pck00011).
* 459 moons: JPL mean elements, calibrated against Horizons (median error 0.58° over 1950–2100), with
  Horizons osculating-element tables for Triton, Titan, Iapetus and the irregular moons (≤0.07°).
* 302 large asteroids/TNOs as full bodies, 75,565 numbered asteroids propagated on the GPU, 4,077
  comets propagated in a Web Worker.
* Every body and star looks like itself: stars get their own colours, texture, active regions,
  prominences, flares and corona from their temperature and size (giant convection cells on red
  supergiants); Vega, Altair, Regulus and four more fast-spinning stars are flattened and tilted
  as interferometers have measured them, and every other star is round; the 459
  moons and the asteroids get 4K maps from the full-resolution USGS mosaics or, where no spacecraft has
  mapped them, a seeded procedural surface and an irregular shape for small bodies; each black hole has
  its own disk temperature, gas pattern and (for M87\* and the microquasars) jets.
* NASA/USGS global maps for 23 bodies, Earth night lights and clouds, Saturn's rings from the Voyager 2
  PPS occultation profile with planet↔ring shadows.
* Free flight with altitude-scaled speed (metres/s to parsecs/s), orbit mode, logarithmic go-to
  autopilot, time control (pause, ×1 … 100 years/s, reverse), labels, orbit lines, search, info panel,
  screenshots.
* Immersive VR (WebXR) designed for the headset: in-headset menu with planet/moon/star browser and
  virtual-keyboard search, laser pointing with hover and haptics, comfortable travel, info cards,
  wrist panel, 3D labels, controllers and hand tracking.
* Visuals: the real Milky Way (NASA SVS Deep Star Maps, Gaia DR2) behind the catalogue stars;
  single-scattering atmospheres (Earth, Mars, Venus, Titan, giant-planet limb haze) with reddened
  sunlight at the terminator; relief from real elevation models (LOLA, MOLA, MESSENGER, ETOPO 2022);
  sun glint on Earth's oceans; 8k maps of Earth, Moon, Mars and Mercury streamed on demand; Saturn,
  Uranus and Neptune from 2025 Hubble OPAL maps; giant-planet and Titan colours from measured albedo
  spectra.
* Black holes: 23 real ones (every dynamically confirmed stellar black hole in BlackCAT, Cygnus X-1,
  Gaia BH1–3, Sagittarius A\*, M87\*) with their companion stars on their real orbits. Light is traced
  past each hole in the Schwarzschild metric per pixel, so the sky behind it bends into Einstein rings,
  the far side of the accretion disk arcs over the shadow, and Doppler beaming brightens the side
  coming towards you. From too far off to make the disk out, a hole is a brilliant point holding
  the light of the disk it grows into; the jets of M87\* and of the microquasars are a glow seen
  through, brighter on the side whose gas comes towards you (their brightness, like the glint's, is
  set for the display: see `docs/notes/black-hole-lighting.md`). Search for them, or open the
  **Deep space** tab in the VR menu.
* The whole Milky Way: a parametric model of the galaxy (thin and thick disks, the boxy bar, four
  spiral arms and the local spur, dust, the nuclear star cluster around Sgr A\*) drives both the
  galaxy's unresolved glow — ray-marched through the dust into a cube map, cross-fading from the
  real NASA map as you leave the Sun's neighbourhood — and **tens of billions of procedural stars**
  generated on demand in Web Workers wherever the catalogues end. Every generated star is
  deterministic (it is always the same star, with a designation like `PS 9.41.-3.0-17` you can
  search for) and none duplicates a catalogued one. Fly to the galactic centre and Sgr A\* sits
  in a blazing star cluster; search "Milky Way" to leave the galaxy and see the barred spiral from
  outside.
* Beyond the Solar System: 6,333 confirmed exoplanets placed around their catalogued stars, a generated
  system around almost every other star, 47 nearby galaxies, 41 nebulae and clusters, and real
  spacecraft on their trajectories.
* Landing: real 3D ground from NASA and NOAA elevation models (sharper patches around landmarks), generated
  hills, craters and shadows elsewhere, walking at eye height, rocky planets of other stars with
  matching terrain and Earth-like skies.
* Saturn's rings from the inside, comets with comas, tails and nuclei, eclipses and moon shadows at
  their real times.
* The ship: special-relativistic motion under live gravity, its own clock, a flip-and-burn
  autopilot that never outruns light; cockpit and chase views, traffic, docking at stations,
  landing, missions and a discovery log.

Known limitations (planned for later phases unless noted):

* Close to a solid world the ground becomes real 3D terrain: elevation models for the Moon, Mars,
  Mercury, Ceres and Earth (about 5–20 km per sample) with generated relief below that, generated relief on
  other round moons, dwarf planets and rocky planets of other stars. Venus, Titan and small
  irregular bodies keep smooth surfaces with relief shading. Earth's clouds are painted on the
  surface from orbit; below 150 km they become a layer 7 km up (the same map, with generated
  billows), which clears again below about 30 km so mountains stay in view (no flying through
  clouds down low), and its ground
  colours come from the Blue Marble map (blurry at eye level; generated rock-and-soil detail is
  added close up).
* Venus uses procedural banding tinted with the Mariner 10 disk colour; the Viking Mars mosaic is
  somewhat over-saturated; OPAL maps miss the latitudes Hubble could not see (filled zonally).
* The Milky Way model is smooth and idealised (arm positions, a clumpy noise for dust and star
  clouds). Other galaxies are procedural discs/spheroids with their catalogued size, type and
  orientation; the nearest one becomes a 3D cloud of generated star clouds and stars (arm positions
  are not the real ones); nebulae are procedural at their catalogued size
  (billboards from afar, a ray-marched volume up close; their shapes are not the real ones);
  globular-cluster stars are generated.
* Terrain shadows are ray-marched per mesh vertex, with the edge placed per pixel (features
  smaller than the mesh spacing cast none). Far from the explorer the mesh is coarse (vertices
  5-10 km apart at 100+ km), so distant crater walls and ridges look blocky. The global elevation models are coarse (5–20 km per
  sample; 0.24–0.9 km in the patches around the landmarks) with generated relief below that, which
  is plausible, not mapped. Generated planets' atmospheres are Earth-like for every temperate world.
* Generated planets are labelled as such; their counts and sizes follow occurrence statistics, not
  observations. Orbit orientations and phases of most real exoplanets are unmeasured and chosen
  deterministically.
* The ship's reactionless drive is fictional (its motion is real). The view from the ship at near light speed is not yet relativistic: the stars are drawn where they
  are, not crowded ahead, bluer and brighter (the instruments say what the view would be). Gravity
  on the ship is Newton's in special relativity, not general relativity: a fast path bends less
  than it would near a star or a black hole.
* The traffic ships and stations are fictional. The ISS and Hubble are propagated from one set of orbital elements
  (2026-10-01), so they drift from their real positions over weeks. Procedural stars beyond the catalogues
  are plausible, not real: their densities, colours and luminosities follow the model.
* Star radii are estimated from V magnitude and temperature with a published bolometric
  correction (Torres 2010), and measured radii are used for a few stars that have them (Proxima
  Centauri, Alpha Centauri A and B, Sirius, Arcturus, Aldebaran, Betelgeuse; CREDITS.md). The
  coolest red dwarfs without one still come out too small, by up to about half. Gaia DR3 places Sirius B 0.033 pc from Sirius A (a known astrometry
  problem for that binary).
* Star faces are stylised: they are drawn after the space observatories' pictures of the Sun
  (false-colour pictures in the extreme ultraviolet, of the hot gas over its surface), not as a
  white-light photograph would show a star (a pale disc with a darker limb and, on the Sun, a few
  dark spots). Their colours are stronger than a star's own pale tint, their brightness is set for
  the display, and their turning and seething are sped up thousands of times. The Sun's pattern is
  real, but it is the Sun of spring 2011, not of the date shown: a NASA map assembled from three
  spacecraft over three weeks, its poles (which no spacecraft has looked down on) generated. Every
  other star's face is generated from its temperature, size and brightness: nobody has seen them.
  The corona's glow and the prominences are artistic in strength.
* Moons without published sizes get a deterministic procedural radius, flagged "estimated".
* Comet comas and tails are a model: their size follows each comet's catalogued total-magnitude
  parameters (M1, K1) and distance from the Sun, the ion tail points straight away from the Sun and
  the dust tail curves back along the orbit; real tails vary with outbursts. The asteroid-belt
  brightness boost for distant asteroids is artistic (physical within 0.05 AU).
* Pluto has no ephemeris outside 1849–2150. Eclipses and moon shadows treat bodies as spheres (up to
  four shadowing bodies at a time), and the coppery light in Earth's shadow is a simple tint.
* VR has no bloom pass (a glare sprite around the Sun stands in) and starts at a lower star magnitude
  limit (6.8, adjustable in Settings) to protect frame rate.
* Atmospheres are single scattering (no multiple scattering); over the landing terrain the haze is
  marched to the real ground, elsewhere to the reference sphere. Skylight on Earth and Mars (what
  fills shadows) is a one-line estimate from the vertical optical depth, not computed per direction. The Milky
  Way map is Earth-centred and fades out beyond ~1 kpc.
* Black holes are non-spinning (Schwarzschild).
* Inside a galaxy its light is kept dim so the view stays readable (the heart of a giant elliptical
  would be a blaze); near the centre of M87 the stars inside it do not show yet. A world made in
  the app is a model like the rest: its size follows its mass by a stated rule (rock as dense as
  the Earth, a giant as big as Jupiter, between the two a line drawn between them, a star of the
  main sequence whose light is all counted as visible); gone on reload. A flick's speed (1,200 px a
  second for the speed of a circle) is reasoned, not yet tuned by hand on a phone.
* Performance has been verified only under software rendering (≈20–30 fps at 720p; ~3–5 ms JS per
  frame). GPU profiling and quality presets come in Phase 6.

2. Planet rendering: cube-sphere quadtree terrain from real DEMs, Bruneton atmospheric scattering,
